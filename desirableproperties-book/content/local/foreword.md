# Foreword

People who run around using phrases like "words of wisdom" always worry me. I have words, but whether they're wise or not is someone else's decision. What I am happy to share is a little of the experience of watching the Internet begin and then blossom, and to try to extract from that some insight into what makes infrastructure possible.

I think we have to agree that this proposal is indeed a broad kind of infrastructure. It is layered on top of the existing Internet, or whatever the Internet morphs into. When you create this sort of overlay, it's important that you use the words *agnostic*, or *technology agnostic*. The Internet Protocol layer is insensitive to the underlying transport, and that was by choice. We didn't care whether it was radio, optical fiber, a dedicated piece of copper or something else. That was helpful: when a new transport or transmission came along, we could simply sweep it in. The IP layer doesn't demand very much. It basically says: can you deliver this bundle of bits with some probability greater than zero?

So this new layer of functionality should be seen as infrastructure, with all of the implications of that.

One implication is standards. It would be hard to overemphasize their importance in an enterprise of this kind, because you want things to interwork, and not everyone who participates will be able to negotiate directly with every other party they might interact with. Standards create an interoperability that would be impossible to attain if you had to have pairwise agreements on how things work. They also allow parties to create competing products and services with consistent properties, so people have choice. And we want broad adoption, because without it, infrastructure doesn't do very much good. Its benefits are directly related to how broadly it's adopted and how widely it's used.

Another lesson we learned very quickly is that there need to be self-sustaining business models. When we started, governments paid for all of it, and the only people allowed to use the Internet had some relationship to one of four agencies. In 1988 I requested permission to connect the MCI Mail commercial email system to the NSFNET backbone. That broke the acceptable use policy, and it was a very deliberate move on my part. In 1989 three commercial Internet service providers popped up. Some of those businesses didn't survive, but there was a business model to support the investment needed not only to spread the infrastructure but to enhance it. We will have to ask the same questions of this one.

I also want to emphasize simplicity. One thing we learned in the Internet Engineering Task Force is that if you're going to do something, try to do it only one way. As soon as you do it two ways, the two parties who want to communicate have to negotiate which way they have chosen. Minimizing complexity is our friend.

Because this infrastructure involves strong authentication and identity, and possibly a basis for transactions of real consequence, financial, medical and others, security, safety and strong authenticity are going to be essential, so that it is resistant to various and sundry forms of attack.

That concern is sharpened by artificial intelligence. My trivial example is the elevator bank where you choose your floor before you get in, and when the door opens there are no controls inside. You are completely dependent on someone else's software. That always makes me nervous, because I used to make a living writing software, or, to be really honest, writing bugs. If you worry about buggy software, you should doubly worry about buggy artificial intelligence, especially large language models that hallucinate. This infrastructure will invite the use of AI to take actions on behalf of users like you and me. That is not necessarily a bad thing; with populations declining or not growing in much of the world, there will be pressure on software to do what people once did. But that means reliability will be a high priority. Humans make mistakes. Software will make mistakes too. We should try very, very hard to reduce the likelihood of a mistake, especially where something is risky.

My two watchwords these days are accountability and agency. For many years I thought anonymity was a good thing, and I still believe that to be largely true. But the Internet and artificial intelligence have become amplifiers of everything, and the problem is the *everything* part: whether it's constructive or harmful, it gets amplified. People who cause a great deal of damage with these tools should be held accountable, and the only way to do that is to identify them. On the flip side, you don't want software, or other people, to take actions on your behalf that you didn't authorize. Strong authentication of you is in your best interest. If software is going to act for us, it must be very hard for it to exhibit bona fides implying that we authorized something we did not. This transitive authority, setting robots to work on our behalf but only doing things we've authorized, needs very careful design. And agency is about the tools we give you and the rest of the infrastructure in order to achieve those desirable properties: that limit the actions taken on your behalf to those you've actually authorized.

When Bob Kahn and I released the design of the Internet publicly in 1974, it was because we went through a little cycle. Who are our allies now? Who were our allies twenty-five years ago? A different list. Who will our allies be twenty-five years from now? We didn't know. So the only way to make sure they would have access was to release it publicly. People ask how we got away with that. We went through that analysis, we released it, and nobody noticed.

Nobody had a map of where this would go. The biggest surprise for me was when the first graphical web interface came along: the stunning fact that the Internet could be visual as well as textual. The next big surprise was the willingness of so many people to put information into the net with no pecuniary motivation. They simply wanted to know that the information they shared was useful to somebody else, and I had no anticipation of that. When the general population got on the Web, we saw a sea change in the diversity of online applications and content. Alongside the smartphone, we have seen a rapidly proliferating array of behaviors, content, incentives and side effects, most of which were not squarely on our radar fifty years ago.

There was no central authority deciding any of it. The Internet is a grand collaboration. People build pieces of it and connect them together on a voluntary basis, and they observe standards developed in a very open and collaborative way. We designed it so that multiple parties could offer service. We didn't say anything about the business models, and we didn't say anything about who could connect to whom: find somebody to connect to, work out the terms, and if you follow the protocols, it will interwork.

What we did decide were its characteristics. It was not a requirement that the network support any particular application, and this is one of the big lessons for me: you don't want to over-specify a system like this. You give it some characteristics, like best-efforts communication, end-to-end recovery, some kind of flow control and the ability to route through multiple networks, but you don't assume any particular application. Because of that, people have been able to build virtually any application that can be carried as a chunk of digital bits. Every over-specification I can think of involves assumptions, and on a system with any longevity at all, those assumptions are guaranteed to be wrong. Leave a lot of room for other choices to be made, and you create a lot of flexibility. The secret sauce was making sure that anybody with a good idea could be heard and could participate.

We also left things out. Authentication is a place where we absolutely did not do very much in the original design, and it turns out that it is sometimes really important to know who you are talking to on the other end. Public key cryptography came too late for us to build into the standards; if it had been more mature, I would have liked to use it. There are lots and lots of policy issues now, of great importance, that were ignored in the Internet's early evolution.

That experience teaches me that when you're inventing something, you should try to think about what the consequences might be. The desirable properties are that thinking, done in advance: not a roadmap for what the next layer will become, which nobody can draw, but the characteristics it must have whatever it becomes.

Finally, context. I once asked a chatbot to write my obituary. It reported that Dr. Cerf had passed away, gave a date much too soon, which I didn't like very much, gave me credit for things other people did and gave them credit for things I did, and then invented family members I don't have. My cartoon answer for how that happens is that the system didn't know this paragraph was about Vint Cerf and that one was about somebody else. Until we figure out how to provide context, we are going to see these anomalies.

So, what needs to happen now is to get into the specifics, figure out what must happen before this can be implemented successfully, and ask ourselves the most important question: what could possibly go wrong? If we don't have answers to questions like that, we are not in a safe spot to implement something as significant as this infrastructure seems to be.

The Desirable Properties in this book are an attempt to answer that question. I hope you will help improve them.

*Vint Cerf*
